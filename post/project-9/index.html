Income–Democracy Nexus: Panel Data Replication

Replicating Acemoglu et al. (2008) using 211 countries (1950–2000) with fixed effects, Anderson-Hsiao IV, and Arellano-Bond GMM estimators.

Project Overview

This project replicates the landmark study by Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson, and Yared (2008) — "Income and Democracy" — which challenges the widely held modernization hypothesis that economic development causes democratization. Using 50 years of panel data across 211 countries, the study applies increasingly rigorous econometric estimators to test whether the income-democracy correlation is causal or spurious.

The Modernization Hypothesis

The modernization hypothesis (Lipset, 1959) predicts that as countries become richer, they naturally transition towards democracy. The cross-sectional correlation between income and democracy is strong and well-documented. But does higher income cause democracy, or do both share common historical roots?

Data

  • Sample: 211 countries, 1950–2000 (unbalanced panel, 5-year intervals).
  • Dependent variable: Freedom House democracy score (0–1).
  • Key regressor: Log GDP per capita (PPP-adjusted).
  • Controls: Lagged democracy, country fixed effects, time fixed effects.

Econometric Methods

  1. Pooled OLS: Baseline cross-sectional regression — reproduces the strong positive correlation seen in the literature.
  2. Fixed Effects (FE): Within-country estimator that eliminates time-invariant country heterogeneity. The income coefficient drops dramatically — Acemoglu et al.'s main finding.
  3. Anderson-Hsiao IV Estimator: Instruments for the lagged dependent variable using deeper lags to address Nickell bias in dynamic FE models.
  4. Arellano-Bond GMM: System GMM using all available lagged levels and differences as instruments — the preferred estimator for dynamic panels with endogeneity.

Key finding: After accounting for country fixed effects, the income-democracy relationship disappears. The OLS correlation is driven by omitted country-level factors (colonial history, geographic endowments, institutional roots) — not a causal income effect.

Implications

  • Economic development alone is not sufficient for democratization — institutional and historical factors dominate.
  • Nickell bias is quantitatively important; GMM provides more reliable estimates than FE for dynamic panels.
  • Rigorous applied econometrics case study combining OLS, FE, IV, and GMM within a coherent research question.
